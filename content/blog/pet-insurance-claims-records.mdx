Pet insurance is one of the few things you buy hoping never to use, then
depend on completely the day you do. In 2024 UK insurers paid out a
record £1.23 billion in pet claims — an average of £685 per claim,
according to the [Association of British Insurers](https://www.abi.org.uk/news/news-articles/2025/5/insurance-payouts-for-pawly-pets-top-1-billion-for-third-year-in-a-row/).
But the claims that make the news are the ones that get *rejected*, and
the reason is almost never the treatment. It is the paperwork.

## Why claims actually fail

Two problems account for most declined claims, and both are about
records rather than medicine:

**Pre-existing conditions.** When you claim, your insurer asks your vet
for your pet's full clinical history — not just the visit you are
claiming for. A limp noted eighteen months ago, a "keep an eye on that
lump" line, a one-off vomiting episode: any of these can be read as the
start of a condition that predates your cover. You cannot rewrite the
vet's notes, but you *can* keep your own dated record so nothing is a
surprise, and so you can challenge a wrong assumption with your own
timeline.

**Incomplete documentation.** The claim form asks for specific
evidence, and a card receipt showing "£340, VETS4U" is not it.

## The records that get a claim paid

Keep these, per pet, from the day you take out a policy:

1. **The policy schedule and terms** — your policy number, start date,
   annual limit, excess, and the wording that says what is and is not
   covered. The number alone is not enough when a query arises.
2. **Itemised invoices**, not totals. Each should show the date, the
   individual line items (consultation, diagnostics, medication,
   procedure) and the practice details. Ask for these at the desk.
3. **Clinical history / vet notes** for anything relevant. You are
   entitled to request your pet's records from your practice.
4. **Referral and diagnostic reports** — blood results, imaging, and any
   specialist letters. Higher-value claims almost always hinge on these.
5. **Proof of preventive care** where the policy requires it. Some
   policies reduce or refuse payouts if vaccinations or dental care were
   not kept up, so your [treatment and vaccination dates](/schedules/dog-worming-schedule)
   can be part of the evidence, not just good practice.
6. **A dated symptom log.** The single most useful thing you can keep:
   when you first noticed something, in your own words. This is what
   lets you contest a "pre-existing" ruling.

## Two habits that prevent most disputes

**Ask for the itemised invoice every single time**, even for a routine
visit. It costs you ten seconds at the desk and is genuinely hard to
reconstruct months later when a condition turns out to be claimable.

**Log symptoms the day you notice them.** Insurers reason from dates. A
contemporaneous note that says "started scratching left ear, 3 March"
carries real weight. A vague memory does not. This is also exactly the
raw material your vet wants — the same logic behind
[what to bring to your pet's next vet visit](/blog/what-to-bring-to-vet-visit).
