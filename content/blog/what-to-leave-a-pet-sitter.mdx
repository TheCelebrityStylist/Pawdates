There is a particular kind of holiday anxiety that has nothing to do
with flights. It is the moment you hand your keys to a sitter and
realise you have thirty seconds to explain a life. Which door sticks,
which pill hides in which treat, what the limp means, who to call.

Do the handover well and you genuinely stop thinking about it. Here is
what actually belongs in it.

## The four things that matter in an emergency

Everything else is comfort. These four are the ones that decide how a
bad night goes. The American Veterinary Medical Association's guidance on
[care while you are away](https://www.avma.org/resources-tools/pet-owners/emergencycare/whos-charge-your-animals-care-while-youre-away)
is blunt about all four:

1. **How to reach you** — every method, not just one number that might
   be on airplane mode.
2. **Your vet and a 24-hour emergency clinic** — names, numbers and
   addresses, because the regular practice may be closed.
3. **A named decision-maker and a spending limit.** Choose someone who
   can authorise treatment if you cannot be reached, and agree how it
   will be paid. The AVMA suggests arranging a payment method in advance
   and even warning your card issuer, so an unusual charge is not
   declined as suspected fraud at the worst possible moment.
4. **What you would refuse.** Be explicit about any treatment you would
   not want authorised without speaking to you first.

## The daily routine

This is what makes the sit calm rather than merely survivable:

- **Feeding** — how much, how often, what times, and what absolutely not
  to give. In a house with more than one animal, say clearly who eats
  what and where, so nobody gets double-fed or eats another pet's diet.
- **Medication** — the drug, the dose, the time, and the trick that
  actually gets it swallowed. Note anything that must be given *with*
  food.
- **Toilet and exercise** — walk times, lead or off-lead, the dog who
  bolts at cats, the litter tray that needs scooping twice a day.
- **Normal versus not-normal** — the behaviour that is just your pet
  being your pet, and the specific signs that mean call the vet. This is
  the hardest thing for a sitter to judge and the most valuable thing you
  can write down.

## Access and household logistics

- Keys, alarm codes, and which door to use.
- Where the food, litter, leads, medication and cleaning kit live.
- A backup human — a neighbour or friend who can get in if the sitter is
  locked out or unwell.
- Bin day, thermostat, the window that must stay shut.

## The mistake almost everyone makes

The instinct is to write everything, and the result is a document the
sitter skims once and never reopens. When the dog is being sick at
midnight, nobody scrolls a twelve-page WhatsApp. Lead with the four
emergency items and the daily routine; keep the deep background
available but collapsed.
