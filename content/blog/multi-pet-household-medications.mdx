One pet is a routine. Two or three is a logistics problem — different
weights, different products, sometimes different species, and usually
more than one human doing the dosing. It works fine right up until the
morning someone grabs the wrong tube, or gives a dose that was already
given. Both are avoidable, and both are more common than owners like to
admit.

## The two failure modes

Almost every multi-pet medication problem is one of these:

**The wrong-animal mix-up.** You reach for a flea spot-on and apply the
dog's to the cat, or the big dog's dose to the small one. This is not a
minor slip. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration, in its guidance on
[veterinary medication errors](https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/product-safety-information/veterinary-medication-errors),
warns to keep each animal's drugs separate and never to share one pet's
medicine with another unless a vet has said it is safe — because a drug
that is fine for a dog can be dangerous for a cat, and dose bands are set
by weight for a reason.

**The double-dose.** One person gives the tablet; the other, not knowing,
gives it again. Or the same treatment gets applied twice because nobody
was sure it had been done. The [AVMA's medication guidance](https://www.avma.org/resources/pet-owners/petcare/your-pets-medications)
is clear on the fix: keep drugs in their original labelled containers and
never assume — confirm.

## Six habits that make it safe

1. **One product, one pet.** Store each animal's medication separately —
   different shelves, different boxes, whatever it takes so you cannot
   grab the wrong one on autopilot.
2. **Keep original labels.** The container tells you the animal, the dose
   and the expiry. A loose tablet in a drawer tells you nothing.
3. **Colour-code.** For homes with several pets, vets often suggest
   colour-coding syringes, boxes or labels per animal. It sounds fussy
   until the morning it saves you.
4. **Never split by eye.** Do not divide a large-dog dose among smaller
   pets. Buy the right product for each weight band.
5. **Log the dose when you give it**, not later. This is the one that
   stops double-dosing dead.
6. **Make the log shared**, so every carer sees the same truth.

## Why the double-dose is really a communication problem

A double-dose is almost never carelessness — it is two careful people
without a shared record. Partner A feeds and flea-treats the cats before
work; Partner B, home first, sees no note and does it again. The
medicine was fine. The system was missing.
