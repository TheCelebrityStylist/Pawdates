Most of what determines whether a vet visit is useful happens before you
leave the house. The vet has maybe fifteen minutes and a patient who
cannot describe symptoms. What you carry through the door — records,
dates, samples and a written list — is the difference between a diagnosis
and a "let's wait and see."

## Bring the history, not just the pet

The single most valuable thing you can bring is an accurate timeline.
The [American Animal Hospital Association](https://www.aaha.org/resources/preparing-your-pet-for-a-successful-veterinary-visit/)
recommends writing your questions and observations down *before* the
appointment, because it is genuinely easy to forget them once you are in
the room with an anxious animal.

- **Dates.** When did the symptom start? Is it daily, or twice a week?
  Getting better or worse? "He's been off his food since Tuesday" tells a
  vet far more than "he hasn't been himself."
- **Previous records** if you are seeing a new practice — vaccination
  history, past diagnoses, recent test results.
- **Current products.** Every medication, supplement, and the flea and
  worming products you use, with exact names and doses. Photos of the
  labels are fine and often better than memory.

## Bring the physical things

- **A sample, if relevant.** For digestive or urinary problems, a fresh
  stool or urine sample can turn a two-visit problem into a one-visit
  one. Collect it close to the appointment and call ahead to check what
  they need.
- **Your pet's current food** — or a photo of the bag. Diet comes up
  constantly, and the ingredient list settles questions quickly.
- **A carrier or secure lead**, and for anxious pets, high-value treats
  and a familiar blanket. A calmer patient is easier to examine
  properly.
- **A video** of anything intermittent. A limp, a cough, a seizure or a
  strange behaviour almost never performs on cue in the exam room. A
  ten-second clip on your phone is worth more than any description.

## The questions that change care

Go in with three or four written questions. The ones worth asking on
almost any visit:

- Is my pet's weight where it should be, and if not, what is the plan?
- Are the vaccinations and parasite prevention up to date, and what is
  next? (Our [worming](/schedules/dog-worming-schedule) and
  [flea](/schedules/dog-flea-treatment-schedule) schedules are handy to
  bring for this.)
- What specific signs should make me call you before the next visit?
- Is there anything in the history we should be keeping an eye on?

## Why this is hard to do from memory

The reason vet-visit prep so often fails is that the information is
scattered. The symptom started three weeks ago and the detail has
faded. The last flea date is in a different app. The weight is a guess.
By the time you are in the waiting room, half of it is gone.
